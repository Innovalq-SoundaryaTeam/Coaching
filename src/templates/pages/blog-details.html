Most aspirants treat every topic in the syllabus as equally important — and that's the single biggest reason study plans fall apart by month two. The truth is that competitive exams reward strategic prioritization far more than raw hours studied. In this article, we break down exactly how our faculty helps students decide what to study first, what to revise weekly, and what to leave for the final month.

Start with a diagnostic, not a textbook

Before opening a single chapter, take a full diagnostic test. The goal isn't to score well — it's to generate a topic-wise accuracy map that tells you, objectively, where your 100 hours of study time will earn the most marks.

"Students who diagnose before they plan consistently outperform students who study more but plan less." — {{ post.author }}

Build a weighted weekly schedule

Once you know your weak topics, allocate study blocks proportionally — not evenly. A topic worth 15 marks that you're scoring 40% on deserves more hours than a topic worth 5 marks you're already scoring 80% on.

  • Week 1–4: Foundation building across all sections
  • Week 5–12: Weighted practice based on diagnostic results
  • Week 13–20: Full-length mock tests + targeted revision
  • Final 4 weeks: Rapid revision + previous year papers only

Review every mock test within 24 hours

The real learning happens after the test, not during it. Spend at least 45 minutes reviewing every incorrect and unattempted question, and log recurring mistake patterns in your performance tracker.
